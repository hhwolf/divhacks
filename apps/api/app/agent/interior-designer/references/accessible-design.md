# Accessible design reference

Figures below come from the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design unless marked otherwise. ADA applies to public buildings, not private homes, but its numbers are the best tested baseline available. Always adjust to the real person: their chair width, their reach, their preferences.

## Wheelchair and mobility aid users

- Turning space: 60 in (152 cm) diameter circle, or a T-shaped space within a 60 in square
- Clear floor space to approach anything (a sink, a closet, a bedside): 30 x 48 in (76 x 122 cm)
- Accessible route width: 36 in (91 cm) clear. Can narrow to 32 in (81 cm) for no more than 24 in of length, like a doorway
- Door clear opening: 32 in (81 cm) minimum with the door open 90 degrees. Offset hinges can gain about 2 in without reframing
- Thresholds: 1/4 in vertical max, or up to 1/2 in if beveled
- Forward reach range: 15 to 48 in (38 to 122 cm) above the floor
- Accessible counter or desk height: 28 to 34 in (71 to 86 cm)
- Knee clearance under a table, desk or sink: 27 in high, 30 in wide, 17 to 25 in deep
- Bed height for easy transfer: roughly 20 to 23 in to the mattress top (common practice, not ADA). Match wheelchair seat height where possible
- Leave the 30 x 48 in clear space beside the bed on the transfer side
- Lever handles instead of knobs. D-pulls instead of small knobs on drawers
- Closet rods: 48 in max height for forward reach. Pull-down rods help
- Bathroom grab bars: 33 to 36 in above the floor
- Toilet centerline: 16 to 18 in from the side wall
- Flooring: firm, stable, slip resistant. Low-pile rug (1/2 in max pile) with secured edges, or no rug

## Blind and low-vision residents

- Consistency matters more than style. Once a layout is learned, don't move furniture without telling the person and walking it with them
- Keep circulation paths completely clear. No cords, baskets or ottomans in walkways
- Protruding objects: anything mounted between 27 and 80 in (69 and 203 cm) above the floor should stick out no more than 4 in (10 cm) into a path, because a cane sweeping at floor level won't find it. Wall sconces, open shelves and floating desks are the usual offenders
- Contrast: make edges visible. Dark furniture on light floors or the reverse, contrasting strips on stair edges, a contrasting countertop edge, switch plates that contrast with the wall. A common guideline is a light reflectance value (LRV) difference of at least 30 points between adjacent surfaces
- Glare: matte and satin finishes over gloss. Diffused light over bare bulbs. Sheer curtains or blinds to control direct sun
- More light overall, and even light. Low-vision users often need 2 to 3 times the light level others do, especially for tasks
- Tactile cues: a change in floor texture near a doorway, a bump dot on the oven dial, a distinct handle on a specific cabinet
- Rounded corners on tables and counters at hip height
- Avoid busy patterns on floors, which can hide objects and read as changes in level

## Aging in place

- One-level living where possible: bedroom, bathroom and kitchen on the same floor
- Chairs with arms and a firm seat about 18 to 19 in high are easier to rise from
- Night lighting: plug-in motion lights on the path from bed to bathroom
- Remove or secure throw rugs, the leading trip hazard at home
- Rocker light switches, and switches at the entry of every room

## How to explain it

Say which constraint drove a decision. "The desk goes on the east wall because that's the only spot with a 60 inch turning circle in front of it." It helps the user trust the plan and adapt it later.

Use everyday words when you explain these: "room to turn a wheelchair around" rather than "turning radius", "nothing sticking out from the wall at waist height" rather than "protruding objects".
